Concepts

A few ideas carry all of tsdb. Each section below is short, and the clients page shows them in code.

Series and points

A series is one sensor: a name such as pump7.flow and a list of points. A point is a time and a value. Times are milliseconds since 1970-01-01 UTC.

You get a series by its name, and tsdb gives you its id: a 64-bit number. Keep the id in a 64-bit integer and look it up by name; never count ids yourself. The first time you ask for a name, the series is created.

Last write wins. Writing a value for a time that already exists replaces it. You never get duplicates back. A range of time is always written [start, end): the start is in it, the end is not.

Value types

A series gets its type when you create it, and keeps it. Series created without a type are float32. Any value may be missing (null); summaries skip nulls.

Your valuesTypeWhy
most sensors: temperatures, pressures, flows, levelsfloat32 (the default)smallest and fastest; about 7 significant digits, more than a sensor delivers
values that need more than 7 significant digits: a totalizer at 12,345,678.9 m³, energy countersfloat64about 15 significant digits
counters and amounts in a fixed unit: liters, Wh, eventsint64exact, sums included
on/off, open/closedbool
modes and status: "running", "stopped", "fault"statestored as small codes, with the names kept once

Store a value in the type it comes in. A float32 reading stored as float64 does not get more precise: 35.1 becomes 35.0999999…, which takes up to 2.6 times the space and is slower to read.

Sums of int64 values are exact. Sums of float values can differ in the last digits from one run to the next, because the order of the additions changes. If exact sums matter, store the value as an int64 in fixed point: 21.3 °C as 21300 milli-degrees.

Raw points

Ask for the points of a time range, oldest first or newest first, with a limit if you like. A limit stops early: "the newest 10 points" of a year of data reads about 10 points.

Buckets

A bucket is a summary of the points in one piece of time: count, sum, min, max and average. You choose how long the pieces are.

  • Fixed buckets have one length, such as 5 minutes or 1 hour, counted from 1970. "The average of every hour" is a fixed bucket of 3,600,000 ms.
  • Calendar groups follow the calendar: day, week (from Monday), month, quarter and year, or several of them (2 weeks). Days, months and years have different lengths, so these are not fixed.

A limit works on buckets too: "the last 10 hours" of a year of data reads about 10 hours of it.

tsdb keeps a summary (count, sum, min, max) of every block of up to 8192 points. A bucket that covers whole blocks is answered from those summaries without reading the points. That is why a count over all the data is almost free, and why buckets smaller than a block cost more. Compression explains the summaries and how the blocks are packed.

Time zones

The database and the server know only UTC. A day in Oslo begins at 22:00 or 23:00 UTC the day before, depending on summer time. So the clients turn your local periods into UTC times for you: you ask for "days in Europe/Oslo", the client computes where each Oslo day starts and ends, and the server sums between those times. The CLI does the same with --tz Europe/Oslo. Embedded C programs use tsdb_query_group_local from tsdb_calendar.h.

Spread

Besides count, sum, min, max and average, a bucket can tell how much the values spread: the range (max − min), and the variance and standard deviation, both for the whole population and as a sample. They stay exact for a sensor with a large offset and a small spread (air pressure: 101325 Pa, ± 5), and are exact for int64 values. The spread costs a second pass over the values, so you ask for it with its own call.

Time-weighted averages (TWA)

A plain average counts every reading once. That is wrong for a sensor that reports irregularly, or only when its value changes: a value that held for an hour should count more than one that held for a second. A time-weighted average weighs each value by how long it held.

  • Step (the default): each value holds until the next reading.
  • Linear: the value runs on a straight line from one reading to the next.
  • Max gap: a silence longer than this counts as unknown, not as the old value. Without it there is no limit.

The value in force at the start of a bucket comes from the last reading before it, however long ago (within the max gap). The last reading of a series holds until a later reading arrives, never up to "now". Each bucket also says how much time it covered. For a bool series, the average is the share of the covered time that it was true. Wide buckets, such as days of minute readings, come mostly from stored summaries.

Downsampling for charts (LTTB)

A chart 1,000 pixels wide does not need a million points. LTTB (largest triangle three buckets) picks at most the number of points you ask for, so that the line keeps its shape: the peaks and dips stay. Every point it returns is a real stored point. The method comes from Sveinn Steinarsson's master's thesis, Downsampling Time Series for Visual Representation (University of Iceland, 2013), which also has his reference code.

Deletes

You can delete the values of one series in a time range [start, end). Queries stop seeing them at once; the disk space comes back later, when background merges rewrite the data.

Replace

Replace deletes a range of one series and writes new points in its place, in one step. A reader sees the old values or the new ones, never a mix, and so does a crash. Use it to correct a range of bad readings.

Retention

Give a series a retention period, such as 30 days, and values older than that disappear on their own. Queries stop returning them at once; merges remove them from disk in the background. The limit is safe on disk when the call returns. Without one, a series keeps its values for ever.

Many series in one request

You can ask about many series at once: the last hour of 200 sensors, or a day of every pump by hour. The server works on the series in parallel and sends the answers back in the order you asked. All series are read at the same instant, so a write to several series shows up in all of them or in none. Each series succeeds or fails on its own: an unknown series does not spoil the others.

Safe writes

In the default mode, a write returns only when it is on disk. Writers that arrive at the same time share one sync (group commit). The server can instead sync every few milliseconds (--wal-sync-ms), which bounds what a power failure can lose to the last few milliseconds of answered writes. We test this by killing the server and by simulating power loss.
